HuskyBook

A peer-to-peer directory connecting UConn students who offer personal-care services with students looking to book one. Built and shipped as a full-stack app, then archived on purpose. The interesting part is an access-control model where privacy is impossible to leak by accident.

Node.jsExpressSQLiteViteREST APIMagic-link auth
Archived September 2026 and no longer deployed. It still runs locally; the source is open and these screenshots are from the working app. Read on for what it did and why I stopped.
What Made It Interesting
Privacy by construction
Exact location and contact never leave the server until a specific request is accepted. It is enforced in two places: a CASE WHEN status = 'accepted' and the serializers, so no route can leak those fields by forgetting to strip them. They are never in the object to strip.
Server-side ownership
Only the account that owns a listing can act on it, checked against the session rather than any id the client supplies.
Tested over real HTTP
32 tests drive a real magic-link sign-in and exercise the privacy and ownership rules end to end, not a faked session.
The stack
A Node and Express REST API (16 endpoints over a 5-table SQLite schema) and a Vite front end, wired as npm workspaces.
The Flow

The public directory. Building and campus zone are shown; exact room numbers and contact details are not, until a request is accepted.

Sending a request. The modal states the privacy rule up front, before you commit to anything: exact location and contact unlock only after the provider accepts.

The provider side. Accepting a request is the exact moment the location unlocks, and only for that one requester.

Why I Stopped

HuskyBook works. It just solves a problem neither side actually has. Unlicensed student providers already book through Instagram and group chats, at zero cost and to an audience they have already built. Licensed businesses already use real booking tools. A directory sitting between the two adds a step for everybody and removes one for nobody.

Charging a fee would have made it worse: routing money for unlicensed services through a platform I run is a liability worth taking on only for a product people are actually asking for, and nobody was. So I stopped here instead of building features nobody needed, and archived it as a finished, honest piece of work rather than a live product chasing users it did not have.
